Sample Preparation

Blood plasma and oral fluid are two common biological matrices used for drug detection. While plasma provides a reliable quantification of drug concentrations, giving a direct measure of circulating drug levels, the collection of plasma can be invasive and require trained personnel. This sampling method is not always ideal in outpatient or resource-limited settings.

Oral fluid has become a favorable alternative, allowing for easy and non-invasive sample collection. Oral samples, such as saliva, reflect free drug fractions and may provide closer approximations to pharmacologically active concentrations. Despite these benefits, challenges arise such as lower analyte concentrations and sample variability, with pH, salivary flow rate, and drug properties influencing oral fluid drug levels. Because of these challenges, it is vital to apply advanced analytical techniques to achieve accurate opioid quantification.

Ionization Source

The first step of mass spectrometry is the ionization source. Electrospray ionization (ESI) ionizes a liquid sample by applying a high voltage to the sample, which passes through a capillary, creating an aerosol of charged droplets. A heated gas then evaporates the solvent, causing the droplets to shrink and divide into smaller droplets. The solvent continues to evaporate until the analyte molecules are in the gas phase and ready to enter the mass analyzer.

ESI is a form of “soft” ionization, which means the molecules being tested do not break apart, and we can determine the structure of the original sample.

Mass Analyzer

The next component of mass spectrometry is the mass analyzer. A triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer is a tandem mass spectrometry instrument that consists of three quadrupole analyzers.

  • The first quadrupole filters ions to select a specific range of mass-to-charge ratios.
  • The second quadrupole is a collision cell.
  • The third quadrupole filters the product ions, delivering them to the detector.

Triple quadrupole mass spectrometers allow for high sensitivity, speed, and efficiency.

Analyte Detection

The final component of mass spectrometry is the detector. An electron multiplier (EM) detector amplifies ion signals, creating a cascade of electrons through secondary electron emission. This creates a measurable electrical signal, which can be converted to a mass spectrum of the sample being tested.
